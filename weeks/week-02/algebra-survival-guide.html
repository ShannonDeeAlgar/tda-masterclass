Before Week 2: Algebra survival guide

The first-year linear algebra that Week 2 will reuse

This page reactivates the first-year linear algebra that Week 2 reuses. It does not introduce chains, boundary maps, cycle spaces or homology. Those are new TDA objects and belong in Week 2.

Scalars and fields

A scalar is a number used to scale a vector. A field is an arithmetic system in which addition, subtraction and multiplication are available, and division is available by every nonzero scalar. The familiar algebraic rules hold.

The rational numbers \(\mathbb Q\), real numbers \(\mathbb R\) and complex numbers \(\mathbb C\) are fields. The integers \(\mathbb Z\) are not a field because, for example, 2 has no multiplicative inverse that is also an integer.

A vector space is always defined over a specified field. Writing \(V=\mathbb R^n\) usually makes the field obvious. In a more abstract vector space, it must be stated.

NoteExample: why the choice of field cannot be omitted

Take the vector \(v=(1,0)\). If the surrounding vector space is \(\mathbb R^2\), then

\[ \sqrt2\,v=(\sqrt2,0) \]

is another vector in the same space. Both \(1/2\) and \(\sqrt2\) are permitted scalars because they are real numbers.

Now consider the rational coordinate plane \(\mathbb Q^2\). The same written vector \(v=(1,0)\) belongs to this space, and \(\tfrac12v=(\tfrac12,0)\) remains in it. However, \(\sqrt2v=(\sqrt2,0)\) does not belong to \(\mathbb Q^2\) because \(\sqrt2\) is not rational. If we tried to call \(\mathbb Q^2\) a vector space over \(\mathbb R\), scalar multiplication would fail to stay inside the set. This violates a vector-space requirement called closure under scalar multiplication.

The field therefore specifies which coefficients are legal when vectors are scaled and combined. State vectors, regression coefficient vectors and perturbations are usually taken over \(\mathbb R\), so this choice is often present without being stated aloud. The phrase “vector space” is incomplete until its permitted scalars are known.

◆ TDA translation. Chains can be added without choosing a field. The coefficient system is still needed because it specifies what the coefficients are and how repeated simplices combine or cancel. Integer coefficients produce groups; field coefficients produce vector spaces. Week 2 begins with a field so that Gaussian elimination, rank and dimension apply directly. The coefficient discussion in Week 2 defines chains only after the underlying simplices have been introduced.

The coefficient field \(\mathbb F_2\)

The course begins with

\[ \mathbb F_2=\{0,1\}. \]

Arithmetic is modulo 2: only parity remains. Thus

\[ 1+1=0, \qquad 1-1=0, \qquad -1=1 \quad\text{in }\mathbb F_2. \]

This does not change ordinary integer arithmetic. It says that two occurrences have even parity, represented by 0 in this two-element field.

Algebraically, \(\mathbb F_2\) is the quotient \(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\): all even integers form the class represented by 0, and all odd integers form the class represented by 1. The notation “modulo 2” means that integers differing by a multiple of 2 are deliberately treated as equivalent. The quotient interpretation is developed again with angles below.

NoteExample: parity as a toggle

Let 0 mean off and 1 mean on. Adding 1 toggles the state. Starting at 0, one toggle gives 1 and a second gives 0. The calculation \(1+1=0\) records this parity rule.

Other coefficient choices

The coefficient field determines which combinations and cancellations homology can distinguish. Common choices include:

Coefficients What the arithmetic permits When the choice may be sensible
\(\mathbb F_2\) Binary coefficients and parity cancellation; orientation signs disappear. A fast, transparent first analysis of components, loops or voids when orientation and torsion are not the question.
\(\mathbb F_p\) for an odd prime \(p\) Arithmetic modulo \(p\), with signs and more than two coefficient values retained. Comparing prime-dependent behaviour or probing torsion that may be visible at a particular prime.
\(\mathbb Q\) Exact rational coefficients and ordinary signs, but no finite-field torsion. Orientation-sensitive calculations when exact field arithmetic is useful and torsion is not required.
\(\mathbb R\) Real coefficients and ordinary signs. Compatibility with real-valued linear algebra or downstream numerical summaries, although it gives the same Betti numbers as \(\mathbb Q\) for finite simplicial complexes.
\(\mathbb C\) Complex coefficients. Problems carrying a natural complex-linear structure; it is uncommon as a default for ordinary TDA homology.

Integer coefficients \(\mathbb Z\) are also important, particularly when torsion and orientation matter, but \(\mathbb Z\) is not a field. The resulting homology objects are abelian groups rather than vector spaces, so rank alone no longer records all their structure. The system \(\mathbb F_p\) is a field only when \(p\) is prime. For example, arithmetic modulo 4 is not a field because the nonzero element 2 has no multiplicative inverse modulo 4.

◆ TDA translation. \(\mathbb F_2\) is a common TDA default because orientation signs disappear, coefficients are binary, reduction is exact, and binary columns can often be stored and reduced efficiently. Many first analyses ask whether components, loops or voids occur without requiring orientation-sensitive information.

This is convenience, not neutrality. Other fields can produce different homology. For example, the real projective plane has a one-dimensional first homology over \(\mathbb F_2\) but zero-dimensional first homology over \(\mathbb Q\). Week 2 uses \(\mathbb F_2\) transparently; an application concerned with orientation or torsion should choose or compare coefficients deliberately.

The coefficient field changes the algebraic lens, not the input representation. “The Betti number” should therefore mean the Betti number over a stated field.

Vectors, bases and coordinate columns

A vector space is a collection of objects that can be added and multiplied by scalars from a specified field. The span of vectors \(v_1,\ldots,v_k\) is the set of all their linear combinations:

\[ \operatorname{span}\{v_1,\ldots,v_k\} = \left\{a_1v_1+\cdots+a_kv_k: a_1,\ldots,a_k\text{ are scalars}\right\}. \]

It is the smallest vector subspace containing those vectors. A basis is a collection of independent vectors whose span is the whole vector space. Every vector then has a unique linear combination of those basis vectors. The dimension of a finite-dimensional vector space is the number of vectors in any basis for that space.

Order is not part of the abstract idea of a basis. It becomes relevant when coefficients are written as a coordinate tuple or placed into matrix rows and columns. For example, relative to the same two basis vectors \(b_1,b_2\), the coefficients \(2b_1+5b_2\) are written \((2,5)^T\) if the order is \((b_1,b_2)\) and \((5,2)^T\) if the order is \((b_2,b_1)\). The vector has not changed; only the bookkeeping order has.

Different bases of the same finite-dimensional vector space contain the same number of vectors, but they are not identical descriptions. Coordinates and matrices generally change with the basis. An invertible change-of-basis matrix translates between them, so no basis is intrinsically the only correct one. Some bases are more useful because they align with coordinates, eigenvectors, modes, sparsity or the objects in a computation.

A vector and its coordinate column are related but not identical:

  • the vector is the underlying object in the vector space;
  • the coordinate column records its coefficients relative to a selected basis and a stated order of that basis.

Changing the basis can change the coordinate column without changing the vector.

NoteExample: one vector in two bases

Use \(\mathbb R^2\) with its standard Cartesian coordinate system. Its standard basis vectors point along the horizontal and vertical coordinate axes:

\[ e_1=(1,0),\qquad e_2=(0,1). \]

The vector \(v=3e_1+e_2\) has standard coordinate column

\[ [v]_{(e_1,e_2)}= \begin{pmatrix}3\\1\end{pmatrix}. \]

Now use the basis

\[ b_1=(1,1),\qquad b_2=(1,-1). \]

Since \(v=2b_1+b_2\), its coordinate column is

\[ [v]_{(b_1,b_2)}= \begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}. \]

The vector has not changed; its numerical description has.

Dynamical systems translation. A physical perturbation is an underlying vector. Its coordinates may be expressed in measured variables, an eigenvector basis or modal coordinates. Changing basis changes the numbers used to describe the perturbation, not the perturbation itself.

◆ TDA translation. Week 2 introduces new vector spaces whose basis elements happen to be vertices, edges or higher-dimensional simplices. Only there will a vector in such a space be named a chain. The Week 2 notes and lecture walkthrough make that translation explicit.

◇ Object check. At this point the objects are an ordinary vector space \(V\), a basis \(B\) and a vector \(v\in V\). To interpret the entries of the coordinate column \([v]_B\), the basis vectors must also be put in a stated order.

Linear maps and matrices

A linear map \(T:V\to W\) satisfies

\[ T(u+v)=T(u)+T(v), \qquad T(av)=aT(v). \]

After bases \(B\) for \(V\) and \(C\) for \(W\) have been chosen and ordered, a matrix represents the map. It may be written

\[ A=[T]_{C\leftarrow B}. \]

The objects have different roles:

  • \(T\) is the underlying linear map, independent of coordinates;
  • \(A\) is the numerical matrix representing \(T\) in the chosen input basis \(B\) and output basis \(C\).

Their relationship is

\[ [T(v)]_C=A[v]_B. \]

Changing either basis can change \(A\) without changing \(T\). Each matrix column is the coordinate column of the output produced by one input basis vector.

The familiar \(2\times2\) format is

\[ A= \begin{pmatrix} a&b\\ c&d \end{pmatrix}, \qquad x= \begin{pmatrix} x_1\\x_2 \end{pmatrix}, \]

with

\[ Ax= \begin{pmatrix} ax_1+bx_2\\ cx_1+dx_2 \end{pmatrix}. \]

In standard Cartesian bases, it is common to identify vectors with their coordinate columns and write \(A=[T]\). The two columns then record

\[ [T(e_1)]=\begin{pmatrix}a\\c\end{pmatrix}, \qquad [T(e_2)]=\begin{pmatrix}b\\d\end{pmatrix}. \]

The two rows determine the first and second output coordinates.

NoteExample: reading a 2 by 2 matrix by columns

Let \(T:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^2\) have the following matrix \(A=[T]\) in the standard Cartesian bases:

\[ A= \begin{pmatrix} 1&1\\ 2&2 \end{pmatrix} \]

in the standard basis. Its columns say

\[ T(e_1)= \begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad T(e_2)= \begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}. \]

Linearity then determines \(T\) on every input. For example,

\[ T\!\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\end{pmatrix} =2T(e_1)-T(e_2) =\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}. \]

Dynamical systems translation. A Jacobian or linearised flow is the coordinate representation of how small perturbations are transported. The underlying linear map is the bigger-picture object; a particular matrix is its description after state variables and bases have been chosen. Reading columns asks how the chosen basis perturbations evolve, while changing coordinates changes the matrix without changing the underlying linear action.

◆ TDA translation. Week 2 first defines two new vector spaces and then defines a boundary map between them. In that setting, the boundary map is the analogue of \(T\). The simplices supply natural bases; ordering those simplices determines which simplex belongs to each row and column of the boundary matrix \(A=[T]_{C\leftarrow B}\). Each matrix column is then the coordinate representation of what the boundary map does to one basis simplex. Follow this construction in the Week 2 boundary-map development.

Kernel, image, rank and nullity

For a linear map \(T:V\to W\), the kernel is

\[ \ker T=\{v\in V:T(v)=0\}. \]

It contains the input directions erased by the map. The image is

\[ \operatorname{im}T=\{T(v):v\in V\}. \]

It contains the output directions the map can produce.

The dimension of the kernel counts the independent input directions that are collapsed to zero. This number is the nullity:

\[ \operatorname{nullity}T=\dim(\ker T). \]

The dimension of the image counts the independent output directions that the map can produce. This number is the rank:

\[ \operatorname{rank}T=\dim(\operatorname{im}T). \]

The rank-nullity theorem states that, for finite-dimensional \(V\),

\[ \dim V=\operatorname{rank}T+\operatorname{nullity}T. \]

The intuition is an accounting of input degrees of freedom. After a suitable basis is chosen, some independent input directions survive as independent output directions and the remaining independent directions lie in the kernel. Rank-nullity says that none of the input dimensions are unaccounted for.

NoteExample: kernel, image and rank of one matrix

For

\[ A= \begin{pmatrix} 1&1\\ 2&2 \end{pmatrix}, \]

solving \(Ax=0\) gives

\[ x=t \begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}. \]

Hence

\[ \ker A=\operatorname{span}\!\left\{ \begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\right\}. \]

The two columns are identical, so every output is a multiple of \((1,2)^T\):

\[ \operatorname{im}A=\operatorname{span}\!\left\{ \begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}\right\}. \]

Equivalently, the map acts as

\[ T(x_1,x_2)= \bigl(x_1+x_2,\,2(x_1+x_2)\bigr). \]

It compresses the whole Cartesian plane onto the line spanned by \((1,2)\). Moving in the input direction \((1,-1)\) changes \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) by equal and opposite amounts, so their sum stays fixed and the output does not change. That direction is erased and spans the kernel.

There is one independent output direction, so the rank is 1. There is one independent erased input direction, so the nullity is 1. The two-dimensional input is therefore accounted for by

\[ 2=1+1. \]

Dynamical systems translation. Kernel and image describe information flow more generally. The kernel contains distinctions in the input that the map cannot transmit; the image is the set of output directions the map can realise. Rank measures how many independent directions survive the map, while nullity measures how many independent directions are lost. This interpretation applies to observation operators, linearisations, reduced models and control maps, not only to a particular matrix calculation.

◆ TDA translation. Week 2 applies kernel and image to adjacent boundary maps. The kernel will be given the new name cycle space, and an adjacent image will be given the new name boundary space. Homology then compares them. Those definitions and the outline-versus-filled triangle belong in Week 2, not in this refresher.

Quotients: one idea that may be less familiar

A quotient treats an agreed kind of difference as irrelevant. This is not ordinary division. If an equivalence relation declares \(v\sim w\), the quotient collects all equivalent objects into one class.

NoteExample: angles modulo one full turn

Angles provide a quotient that is used routinely without being named as one. The angles

\[ 0,\qquad 2\pi,\qquad -2\pi,\qquad 4\pi \]

are different real numbers but represent the same direction. More generally,

\[ \theta\sim\phi \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \theta-\phi=2\pi k \text{ for some }k\in\mathbb Z. \]

Working “modulo \(2\pi\)” replaces every collection of angles differing by whole turns with one angle class. The quotient is written \(\mathbb R/(2\pi\mathbb Z)\) and represents the circle of directions.

In this algebraic sense, working modulo something is always quotienting by a stated equivalence: differences belonging to the specified subgroup are treated as irrelevant. Thus integers modulo \(n\) form the quotient \(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z\), where numbers differing by a multiple of \(n\) belong to the same class. In elementary arithmetic, “take the remainder” chooses a convenient representative of that class. The quotient is the full class, not merely the selected remainder. Informal phrases such as “modulo numerical error” are looser language and need not name a formal quotient.

NoteExample: identifying states related by a symmetry

Suppose a rotationally symmetric system treats states \(x\) and \(R_\theta x\) as equivalent for every rotation \(R_\theta\). The quotient does not claim those state vectors are identical. It constructs a reduced description in which their rotational difference is deliberately ignored.

Dynamical systems translation. Symmetry reduction is a quotient construction. Many full states can correspond to one reduced state because the chosen symmetry direction is no longer distinguished.

◆ TDA translation. Week 2 introduces homology as a quotient only after cycles and boundaries have been defined. Its interpretation is that cycles differing by an available boundary represent the same class. Follow that argument in the Week 2 triangle example; it is new TDA content rather than assumed first-year algebra.

## Linear maps and matrices

A **linear map** $T:V\to W$ satisfies

$$
T(u+v)=T(u)+T(v),
\qquad
T(av)=aT(v).
$$

After bases $B$ for $V$ and $C$ for $W$ have been chosen **and ordered**, a **matrix** represents the map. It may be written

$$
A=[T]_{C\leftarrow B}.
$$

The objects have different roles:

- $T$ is the underlying linear map, independent of coordinates;
- $A$ is the numerical matrix representing $T$ in the chosen input basis $B$ and output basis $C$.

Their relationship is

$$
[T(v)]_C=A[v]_B.
$$

Changing either basis can change $A$ without changing $T$. Each matrix column is the coordinate column of the output produced by one input basis vector.

The familiar $2\times2$ format is

$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
a&b\\
c&d
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
x=
\begin{pmatrix}
x_1\\x_2
\end{pmatrix},
$$

with

$$
Ax=
\begin{pmatrix}
ax_1+bx_2\\
cx_1+dx_2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

In standard Cartesian bases, it is common to identify vectors with their coordinate columns and write $A=[T]$. The two columns then record

$$
[T(e_1)]=\begin{pmatrix}a\\c\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
[T(e_2)]=\begin{pmatrix}b\\d\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The two rows determine the first and second output coordinates.

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Example: reading a 2 by 2 matrix by columns"}
Let $T:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^2$ have the following matrix $A=[T]$ in the standard Cartesian bases:

$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1\\
2&2
\end{pmatrix}
$$

in the standard basis. Its columns say

$$
T(e_1)=
\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
T(e_2)=
\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Linearity then determines $T$ on every input. For example,

$$
T\!\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\end{pmatrix}
=2T(e_1)-T(e_2)
=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
:::

::: {.applied-translation}
**Dynamical systems translation.** A Jacobian or linearised flow is the coordinate representation of how small perturbations are transported. The underlying linear map is the bigger-picture object; a particular matrix is its description after state variables and bases have been chosen. Reading columns asks how the chosen basis perturbations evolve, while changing coordinates changes the matrix without changing the underlying linear action.
:::

## Kernel, image, rank and nullity

For a linear map $T:V\to W$, the **kernel** is

$$
\ker T=\{v\in V:T(v)=0\}.
$$

It contains the input directions erased by the map. The **image** is

$$
\operatorname{im}T=\{T(v):v\in V\}.
$$

It contains the output directions the map can produce.

The **dimension of the kernel** counts the independent input directions that are collapsed to zero. This number is the **nullity**:

$$
\operatorname{nullity}T=\dim(\ker T).
$$

The **dimension of the image** counts the independent output directions that the map can produce. This number is the **rank**:

$$
\operatorname{rank}T=\dim(\operatorname{im}T).
$$

The **rank-nullity theorem** states that, for finite-dimensional $V$,

$$
\dim V=\operatorname{rank}T+\operatorname{nullity}T.
$$

The intuition is an accounting of input degrees of freedom. After a suitable basis is chosen, some independent input directions survive as independent output directions and the remaining independent directions lie in the kernel. Rank-nullity says that none of the input dimensions are unaccounted for.

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Example: kernel, image and rank of one matrix"}
For

$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1\\
2&2
\end{pmatrix},
$$

solving $Ax=0$ gives

$$
x=t
\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Hence

$$
\ker A=\operatorname{span}\!\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\right\}.
$$

The two columns are identical, so every output is a multiple of $(1,2)^T$:

$$
\operatorname{im}A=\operatorname{span}\!\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}\right\}.
$$

Equivalently, the map acts as

$$
T(x_1,x_2)=
\bigl(x_1+x_2,\,2(x_1+x_2)\bigr).
$$

It compresses the whole Cartesian plane onto the line spanned by $(1,2)$. Moving in the input direction $(1,-1)$ changes $x_1$ and $x_2$ by equal and opposite amounts, so their sum stays fixed and the output does not change. That direction is erased and spans the kernel.

There is one independent output direction, so the rank is 1. There is one independent erased input direction, so the nullity is 1. The two-dimensional input is therefore accounted for by

$$
2=1+1.
$$
:::

::: {.applied-translation}
**Dynamical systems translation.** Kernel and image describe information flow more generally. The kernel contains distinctions in the input that the map cannot transmit; the image is the set of output directions the map can realise. Rank measures how many independent directions survive the map, while nullity measures how many independent directions are lost. This interpretation applies to observation operators, linearisations, reduced models and control maps, not only to a particular matrix calculation.
:::

Those definitions and the outline-versus-filled triangle belong in [Week 2](index.qmd#examples), not in this refresher.
:::

## Quotients: one idea that may be less familiar

A **quotient** treats an agreed kind of difference as irrelevant. This is not ordinary division. If an equivalence relation declares $v\sim w$, the quotient collects all equivalent objects into one class.

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Example: angles modulo one full turn"}
Angles provide a quotient that is used routinely without being named as one. The angles

$$
0,\qquad 2\pi,\qquad -2\pi,\qquad 4\pi
$$

are different real numbers but represent the same direction. More generally,

$$
\theta\sim\phi
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\theta-\phi=2\pi k
\text{ for some }k\in\mathbb Z.
$$

Working “modulo $2\pi$” replaces every collection of angles differing by whole turns with one angle class. The quotient is written $\mathbb R/(2\pi\mathbb Z)$ and represents the circle of directions.

In this algebraic sense, working modulo something is always quotienting by a stated equivalence: differences belonging to the specified subgroup are treated as irrelevant. Thus integers modulo $n$ form the quotient $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$, where numbers differing by a multiple of $n$ belong to the same class. In elementary arithmetic, “take the remainder” chooses a convenient representative of that class. The quotient is the full class, not merely the selected remainder. Informal phrases such as “modulo numerical error” are looser language and need not name a formal quotient.
:::

::: {.callout-note collapse="true" title="Example: identifying states related by a symmetry"}
Suppose a rotationally symmetric system treats states $x$ and $R_\theta x$ as equivalent for every rotation $R_\theta$. The quotient does not claim those state vectors are identical. It constructs a reduced description in which their rotational difference is deliberately ignored.
:::

::: {.applied-translation}
**Dynamical systems translation.** Symmetry reduction is a quotient construction. Many full states can correspond to one reduced state because the chosen symmetry direction is no longer distinguished.
:::

Its interpretation is that cycles differing by an available boundary represent the same class. Follow that argument in the [Week 2 triangle example](index.qmd#examples); it is new TDA content rather than assumed first-year algebra.
:::
