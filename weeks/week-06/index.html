Where this week sits. A persistence diagram is already a summary of a chosen filtration. Week 6 asks what happens next: compare diagrams directly, reduce them to scalars or functions, or vectorise them for statistics and machine learning. Each route preserves and discards different information.

Learning goals

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

  1. explain the different questions answered by bottleneck distance, Wasserstein distance, Betti curves and vectorised persistence summaries, and state what a stability bound guarantees;
  2. inspect a comparison or summary used in a paper and state what information from the original diagrams it retains and forgets;
  3. choose a summary because it matches the scientific comparison, not merely because it is convenient for software or machine learning;
  4. interpret an observed topological statistic relative to an explicit null or surrogate mechanism; and
  5. compare the topological result with a simpler non-topological baseline before claiming added value.

Expert extension: when points have labels or types

NoteOptional: high-level idea is sufficient

Retain the idea that ordinary persistence forgets labels. The specialised chromatic constructions can be skipped unless labelled populations are part of the scientific question.

Ordinary point-cloud persistence sees locations and distances, but it does not know that one point is a predator and another prey, one cell belongs to the tumour and another to the immune system, or two agents have different roles. If the labels are scientifically meaningful, forgetting them before building a complex can erase the question of interest.

Write a labelled point cloud as \((X,c)\), where

\[ c:X\longrightarrow\{1,\ldots,m\} \]

assigns one of \(m\) colours or types to each point. Chromatic persistence builds filtrations that retain this labelling and can ask how features made by one colour sit inside or interact with features made by all colours (Cultrera di Montesano et al. 2025). Image, kernel and cokernel persistence are specialist ways to separate those contributions; their algebra is not required here.

Worked example: the geometry is the same but the interaction is not

Place red and blue points on the same two noisy rings. In one dataset the colours are thoroughly mixed; in another, the inner ring is red and the outer ring blue. If the colour labels are deleted, the two unlabelled point clouds can be identical and therefore have identical ordinary persistence diagrams. The chromatic question is different: which colour contributes to, surrounds, or shares each feature?

This is a change to the construction, not another vectorisation of an ordinary persistence diagram. The participant practical includes a small visual experiment showing exactly what the unlabelled analysis forgets. Full chromatic alpha computation is an optional research extension.

Why a persistence diagram is awkward for ordinary data analysis

A persistence diagram is an unordered multiset

\[D=\{(b_i,d_i)\}_{i=1}^{n_D}.\]

Different observations can produce different numbers \(n_D\) of points. There is no meaningful “first point” or “second point”, and some death coordinates may be infinite. Most familiar regression, classification and clustering software instead expects each observation to be one fixed-length vector in \(\mathbb R^m\) with consistently ordered coordinates.

Flattening the diagram into \((b_1,d_1,b_2,d_2,\ldots)\) would depend on an arbitrary ordering and fail when diagrams have different cardinalities. Specialised set-valued methods and diagram kernels exist, but standard vector-based machine learning requires a representation step.

Route Output Useful when Main information lost
Direct matching one distance between two diagrams comparing diagrams without vectorising which mechanism produced each discrepancy
Scalar or curve one number or one function interpretation, plotting and simple statistics much of the birth-death configuration
Fixed-length vector vector in \(\mathbb R^m\) regression, classification and clustering exact points, depending on smoothing and discretisation

Direct diagram distances

Let \(D\) and \(E\) be persistence diagrams augmented with diagonal points of unlimited multiplicity, and let \(\Gamma(D,E)\) denote admissible bijections. Using the \(L_\infty\) ground cost,

\[ \lVert(b,d)-(b',d')\rVert_\infty =\max\{|b-b'|,|d-d'|\}. \]

The bottleneck distance is

\[ d_B(D,E)=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(D,E)} \sup_{x\in D}\lVert x-\gamma(x)\rVert_\infty. \]

For each matching, find its worst cost; then choose the matching whose worst cost is smallest. Bottleneck distance therefore records the largest unavoidable discrepancy.

For \(1\leq q<\infty\), the \(q\)-Wasserstein distance is

\[ W_q(D,E)= \left( \inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(D,E)} \sum_{x\in D}\lVert x-\gamma(x)\rVert_\infty^q \right)^{1/q}. \]

It uses the same matching idea but accumulates every cost. Many modest discrepancies can therefore make \(W_q\) large even when no single discrepancy dominates. State \(q\), the ground metric and the treatment of essential points; “Wasserstein distance” alone is incomplete.

Matching \((b,d)\) to the diagonal costs

\[ \inf_{t\in\mathbb R}\lVert(b,d)-(t,t)\rVert_\infty =\frac{d-b}{2}. \]

Worked example: a two-point matching by hand

Let

\[D=\{(0,2),(1,1.4)\},\qquad E=\{(0.1,2.1)\}.\]

Using the \(L_\infty\) ground metric, matching the long intervals costs \(0.1\). The short point \((1,1.4)\) can be matched to the diagonal. Its distance to the diagonal is half its persistence:

\[\frac{1.4-1}{2}=0.2.\]

The bottleneck cost of this matching is the worst individual cost, \(\max(0.1,0.2)=0.2\). A Wasserstein cost aggregates the individual costs, so it responds to the accumulation of many modest differences. The optimisation searches over all admissible matchings, including diagonal matches. The diagram metric, ground metric, Wasserstein order and treatment of essential classes must be stated.

† Qualification. These objects compare or vectorise persistence diagrams. They do not, by themselves, recover the maps between persistence modules or establish the identity of a feature through external time.

Stable does not mean automatically interpretable, and vectorised does not mean scientifically meaningful.

Why diagram comparison is defensible: stability

The bottleneck distance would be of limited value if an arbitrarily small input perturbation could produce an arbitrarily large diagram change. A stability theorem supplies the missing guarantee for a specified class of inputs and filtrations.

Fix a homological dimension \(p\) and coefficient field \(\mathbb F\). Let \(X\) be a triangulable space and let \(f,g:X\to\mathbb R\) be tame functions. Filter the same space by sublevel sets. The classical bottleneck stability theorem states

\[ d_B\!\left(D_p(f),D_p(g)\right) \leq \lVert f-g\rVert_\infty, \qquad \lVert f-g\rVert_\infty=\sup_{x\in X}|f(x)-g(x)|. \]

In words: if the value of the filtration function changes by at most \(\delta\) everywhere, the persistence diagram changes by at most \(\delta\) in bottleneck distance. This prevents a controlled small perturbation from causing an unbounded change in the output.

Worked example: read the bound before the hypotheses

If \(|f(x)-g(x)|\leq0.1\) for every \(x\), the theorem guarantees a diagram matching whose worst cost is at most \(0.1\). A point of persistence greater than \(0.2\) lies more than \(0.1\) from the diagonal, so it cannot disappear by being matched to the diagonal under this perturbation. This does not create a canonical identity for the feature; it provides a bound on diagram change.

NoteExpert-level detail: theorem hypotheses

The technical hypotheses can be skipped on a first pass, but they cannot be deleted from a formal claim. In the classical theorem, triangulability gives a well-behaved combinatorial setting and tameness ensures finitely controlled homological change. Modern statements use alternatives such as q-tameness. The theorem compares two functions on the same domain, using the same sublevel construction, homological dimension and coefficient field.

Two unrelated sampled point clouds do not satisfy that setup automatically. Rips and Čech stability results instead compare metric spaces under their own distances, constants and assumptions (Chazal et al. 2014).

◷ Historical context. Persistence and its computation were developed before the classical stability theorem. Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner and Harer then showed that controlled sup-norm changes in a tame function produce controlled bottleneck changes in its persistence diagram (Cohen-Steiner et al. 2007). Stability did not begin the field by itself; it made robustness to a specified perturbation mathematically explicit.

Data-analysis translation. Stability is not significance or validity. It bounds output change for a permitted input change. It does not justify the metric, prove that a feature is surprising under a null, or establish that a topological feature improves prediction.

Scalar summaries: one number per diagram

For finite bars with lengths \(\ell_i=d_i-b_i>0\), common summaries include

\[ \text{maximum persistence}=\max_i\ell_i, \qquad \text{total persistence of order }q=\sum_i\ell_i^q, \]

and the thresholded count \(\#\{i:\ell_i>\tau\}\). They are easy to compare with a null distribution or use as predictors, but diagrams with very different configurations can share the same scalar.

The persistence entropy normalises the finite bar lengths:

\[ L=\sum_i\ell_i, \qquad p_i=\frac{\ell_i}{L}, \qquad H_{\mathrm{pers}}(D)=-\sum_i p_i\log p_i. \]

Entropy is small when persistence is concentrated in a few dominant bars and larger when it is spread more evenly. Lengths \((4,1,1)\) have lower entropy than \((2,2,2)\), although both have total persistence 6. Rescaling every length by the same factor leaves entropy unchanged, so entropy describes the distribution of persistence, not its overall magnitude. State how infinite and zero-length bars are treated. See Chintakunta and collaborators for the entropy-based barcode construction (Chintakunta et al. 2015).

Worked example: why one scalar is not the diagram

Length collections \((4,1,1)\) and \((4,2,2,2)\) have the same maximum persistence. The maximum ignores the different secondary structure. Total persistence and entropy notice a difference, but neither retains where the bars were born. Choose the statistic to match a pre-stated claim rather than selecting whichever statistic separates the groups afterwards.

Betti curves: the number alive at every scale

For barcode intervals \([b_i,d_i)\), the degree-\(p\) Betti curve is

\[ \beta_p(a)=\sum_i\mathbf 1_{[b_i,d_i)}(a) =\#\{i:b_i\leq a<d_i\}. \]

At each filtration value, it counts how many classes are alive. Curves can be plotted on a common grid, integrated, averaged or passed to functional-data methods. They retain counts through scale but forget which birth is paired with which death.

Worked example: different barcodes, the same Betti curve

\[ \mathcal B=\{[0,2),[1,3)\}, \qquad \mathcal B'=\{[0,3),[1,2)\}. \]

Both curves equal 1 on \([0,1)\), 2 on \([1,2)\) and 1 on \([2,3)\). The Betti curves are identical even though the birth-death pairings differ.

Vectorised persistence: fixed features for statistics and machine learning

Persistence landscapes

Each interval produces a tent function

\[ \lambda_i(t)=\max\{0,\min(t-b_i,d_i-t)\}. \]

The landscape \(\lambda_k(t)\) is the \(k\)th-largest tent value at \(t\). Sampling the first \(K\) landscape levels on a common grid gives a fixed-length vector; retaining them as functions permits means, norms and functional statistics. Bubenik introduced landscapes to place persistence summaries in a vector space suitable for statistical analysis (Bubenik 2015). The grid, number of levels and norm remain choices.

Persistence images

Transform each finite point to birth-persistence coordinates,

\[ (b_i,d_i)\longmapsto z_i=(b_i,p_i), \qquad p_i=d_i-b_i. \]

Choose a weight \(w(p)\) and a smoothing kernel, commonly Gaussian:

\[ \rho(z)=\sum_i w(p_i)\,\phi_\sigma(z-z_i). \]

For grid pixels \(P_j\), define

\[ \mathrm{PI}_j=\int_{P_j}\rho(z)\,dz. \]

The fixed-size array can be flattened for ordinary vector-based learning. Bandwidth, bounds, resolution and weighting are modelling choices: smoothing merges nearby information and clipping the grid discards points. Adams and collaborators introduced persistence images and demonstrated them with vector-based learning, including parameter inference for the linked twist map and anisotropic Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation (Adams et al. 2017).

Dynamical systems interpretation. A persistence image can turn each trajectory window or parameter trial into a vector of equal length for a classifier or regressor. The fitted model concerns the complete pipeline: representation, metric, filtration, homology degree, image bounds, bandwidth and grid. Predictive success does not make those choices neutral.

Practical: compare, vectorise and validate one diagram family

The lecture walkthrough and participant practical carry one noisy-circle example through the complete sequence below. The protocol is part of the practical rather than a detached checklist.

Stage 1: compare diagrams directly

Construct \(H_1\) diagrams for a base circle, a slightly perturbed copy and a coordinate-shuffled surrogate. Predict that the perturbation should be closer to the base than the surrogate. Compute both bottleneck and Wasserstein distances, then inspect whether one large discrepancy or many modest discrepancies explain their ordering.

Stage 2: produce analysis-ready summaries

For every diagram, compute maximum persistence, total persistence, persistence entropy and a Betti curve on a common filtration grid. Construct a persistence image using fixed bounds, bandwidth, resolution and weighting. Record the output shape and identify what each representation has forgotten. The participant notebook requires these choices before comparing groups.

Stage 3: validate one preselected statistic

  1. Select maximum persistence before generating surrogates.
  2. State that coordinate shuffling preserves the separate \(x\) and \(y\) marginals but destroys their pairing and circular geometry.
  3. Generate every surrogate through the complete point-cloud-to-diagram pipeline.
  4. Compare the observed value with the surrogate distribution and with a simpler radial or covariance baseline.
  5. Repeat under one upstream choice, such as sample size or noise level.

The empirical comparison is evidence relative to this teaching null, not a universal test of “topological significance”.

Dynamical systems translation. For a scalar trajectory, independently shuffling samples destroys temporal dependence as well as any reconstructed geometry. If the scientific null is “a linear stochastic process with this spectrum”, a phase-randomised surrogate may be more appropriate because it retains the power spectrum while disrupting selected nonlinear phase relations. If the marginal distribution must also be retained, an amplitude-adjusted Fourier surrogate may be considered. These are examples, not defaults: the appropriate surrogate is determined by the dynamical claim and by exactly which structure should survive the randomisation.

† Qualification. An empirical exceedance proportion becomes a valid inferential quantity only under a justified randomisation or sampling scheme. Producing many surrogate barcodes does not supply that justification.

larger when it is spread more evenly. Lengths $(4,1,1)$ have lower entropy than
$(2,2,2)$, although both have total persistence 6. Rescaling every length by
the same factor leaves entropy unchanged, so entropy describes the
distribution of persistence, not its overall magnitude. State how infinite
and zero-length bars are treated. See Chintakunta and collaborators for the
entropy-based barcode construction [@chintakunta2015entropy].

:::: {.worked-example}
### Worked example: why one scalar is not the diagram

Length collections $(4,1,1)$ and $(4,2,2,2)$ have the same maximum
persistence. The maximum ignores the different secondary structure. Total
persistence and entropy notice a difference, but neither retains where the
bars were born. Choose the statistic to match a pre-stated claim rather than
selecting whichever statistic separates the groups afterwards.
::::

## Betti curves: the number alive at every scale

For barcode intervals $[b_i,d_i)$, the degree-$p$ Betti curve is

$$
\beta_p(a)=\sum_i\mathbf 1_{[b_i,d_i)}(a)
=\#\{i:b_i\leq a<d_i\}.
$$

At each filtration value, it counts how many classes are alive. Curves can be
plotted on a common grid, integrated, averaged or passed to functional-data
methods. They retain counts through scale but forget which birth is paired
with which death.

:::: {.worked-example}
### Worked example: different barcodes, the same Betti curve

$$
\mathcal B=\{[0,2),[1,3)\},
\qquad
\mathcal B'=\{[0,3),[1,2)\}.
$$

Both curves equal 1 on $[0,1)$, 2 on $[1,2)$ and 1 on $[2,3)$. The Betti
curves are identical even though the birth-death pairings differ.
::::

## Vectorised persistence: fixed features for statistics and machine learning

### Persistence landscapes

Each interval produces a tent function

$$
\lambda_i(t)=\max\{0,\min(t-b_i,d_i-t)\}.
$$

The landscape $\lambda_k(t)$ is the $k$th-largest tent value at $t$. Sampling
the first $K$ landscape levels on a common grid gives a fixed-length vector;
retaining them as functions permits means, norms and functional statistics.
Bubenik introduced landscapes to place persistence summaries in a vector
space suitable for statistical analysis [@bubenik2015landscapes]. The grid,
number of levels and norm remain choices.

### Persistence images

Transform each finite point to birth-persistence coordinates,

$$
(b_i,d_i)\longmapsto z_i=(b_i,p_i),
\qquad p_i=d_i-b_i.
$$

Choose a weight $w(p)$ and a smoothing kernel, commonly Gaussian:

$$
\rho(z)=\sum_i w(p_i)\,\phi_\sigma(z-z_i).
$$

For grid pixels $P_j$, define

$$
\mathrm{PI}_j=\int_{P_j}\rho(z)\,dz.
$$

The fixed-size array can be flattened for ordinary vector-based learning.
Bandwidth, bounds, resolution and weighting are modelling choices: smoothing
merges nearby information and clipping the grid discards points. Adams and
collaborators introduced persistence images and demonstrated them with
vector-based learning, including parameter inference for the linked twist map
and anisotropic Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation [@adams2017images].

::: {.applied-translation}
**Dynamical systems interpretation.** A persistence image can turn each
trajectory window or parameter trial into a vector of equal length for a
classifier or regressor. The fitted model concerns the complete pipeline:
representation, metric, filtration, homology degree, image bounds, bandwidth
and grid. Predictive success does not make those choices neutral.
:::

## Practical: compare, vectorise and validate one diagram family

The [lecture walkthrough](solutions.ipynb) and
[participant practical](lab.ipynb) carry one noisy-circle example through the
complete sequence below. The protocol is part of the practical rather than a
detached checklist.

:::: {.worked-example}
### Stage 1: compare diagrams directly

Construct $H_1$ diagrams for a base circle, a slightly perturbed copy and a
coordinate-shuffled surrogate. Predict that the perturbation should be closer
to the base than the surrogate. Compute both bottleneck and Wasserstein
distances, then inspect whether one large discrepancy or many modest
discrepancies explain their ordering.
::::

:::: {.worked-example}
### Stage 2: produce analysis-ready summaries

For every diagram, compute maximum persistence, total persistence,
persistence entropy and a Betti curve on a common filtration grid. Construct a
persistence image using fixed bounds, bandwidth, resolution and weighting.
Record the output shape and identify what each representation has forgotten.
The participant notebook requires these choices before comparing groups.
::::

:::: {.worked-example}
### Stage 3: validate one preselected statistic

1. Select maximum persistence before generating surrogates.
2. State that coordinate shuffling preserves the separate $x$ and $y$
   marginals but destroys their pairing and circular geometry.
3. Generate every surrogate through the complete point-cloud-to-diagram
   pipeline.
4. Compare the observed value with the surrogate distribution and with a
   simpler radial or covariance baseline.
5. Repeat under one upstream choice, such as sample size or noise level.

The empirical comparison is evidence relative to this teaching null, not a
universal test of “topological significance”.
::::

::: {.applied-translation}
**Dynamical systems translation.** For a scalar trajectory, independently shuffling samples destroys temporal dependence as well as any reconstructed geometry. If the scientific null is “a linear stochastic process with this spectrum”, a phase-randomised surrogate may be more appropriate because it retains the power spectrum while disrupting selected nonlinear phase relations. If the marginal distribution must also be retained, an amplitude-adjusted Fourier surrogate may be considered. These are examples, not defaults: the appropriate surrogate is determined by the dynamical claim and by exactly which structure should survive the randomisation.
:::

::: {.qualification}
**† Qualification.** An empirical exceedance proportion becomes a valid
inferential quantity only under a justified randomisation or sampling
scheme. Producing many surrogate barcodes does not supply that
justification.
:::
